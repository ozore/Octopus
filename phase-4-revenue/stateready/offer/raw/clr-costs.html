    Contractor License Costs Ranked: The Cheapest and Most Expensive States

    By Gabriel Giner, Editor  ·  Published 2026-04-23

    One of the most common questions we receive is deceptively simple: how much does it cost to get a contractor license? The honest answer is that it depends enormously on where you are. The total out-of-pocket cost to obtain a general contractor license in the United States ranges from under $200 in the most affordable states to well over $1,500 in the most expensive — and that is before you factor in the ongoing costs of bonding, insurance, and renewal fees that you will pay for as long as you hold the license.

    We went through our verified data for all 50 states and the District of Columbia and calculated the true first-year cost of a general contractor license in every jurisdiction that requires one. Here is what the numbers actually look like.

    What we counted (and what we did not)

    To make these comparisons meaningful, we standardized our cost calculation across all states. For each jurisdiction, we added up:

    • Application fee — the non-refundable fee paid when submitting the initial license application
    • Examination fee — the fee charged by the exam provider (PSI, Prometric, or the state board itself) for the required licensing exam(s)
    • Initial license fee — the fee paid to have the license issued after passing all requirements
    • Surety bond premium — the annual premium on the required contractor bond (typically 1% to 3% of the bond amount for contractors with good credit)
    • Fingerprinting and background check — where required by the state

    We did not include general liability insurance premiums or workers' compensation insurance premiums, because these costs vary dramatically based on business size, payroll, claims history, and the specific coverage limits chosen. We also excluded the cost of study materials, exam prep courses, and the indirect cost of time spent preparing. The numbers below represent the minimum hard costs that every applicant must pay directly to government agencies, exam providers, and surety companies.

    The most affordable states

    Several states keep the barrier to entry remarkably low. In these jurisdictions, a contractor with the required experience can go from application to license for a few hundred dollars or less.

    States with no statewide license requirement

    Before looking at costs, it is important to note that a handful of states do not require a statewide general contractor license at all. In these states — including Texas, Pennsylvania, Kansas, Missouri, Vermont, and a few others — there is no state-level application fee, exam fee, or bonding requirement for general contractors. Licensing, where it exists, is handled by cities and counties, and the costs vary by municipality. A general contractor in these states can legally operate statewide without any state licensing cost, though municipal registration fees may apply.

    Low-cost states (under $300 total)

    Among states that do require a license, several keep total first-year costs under $300:

    • Montana — Montana's registration-based system for general contractors has some of the lowest fees in the country. The application and initial registration combined typically come in under $200, and there is no state-administered exam for the general contractor classification.
    • Idaho — Idaho requires registration with the Idaho Contractors Board, and the total out-of-pocket including registration fee and the public works contractor license (where applicable) stays well under $300 for most applicants.
    • Maine — While Maine does not require a general contractor license for most residential and commercial work, contractors who need to register can do so for minimal fees. Specialty trades like electrical and plumbing have separate (and higher) licensing costs.
    • Wyoming — Wyoming's contractor registration process is straightforward and inexpensive, with total government-imposed fees generally under $250.

    The middle of the pack

    Most states fall in the $400 to $800 range for total first-year licensing costs. These states typically require a formal application, one or two exams, a surety bond, and a background check, but the individual fees are moderate.

    Mid-range states ($400–$800 total)

    • Georgia — The Georgia Division of Professional Licensing charges a $100 application fee, and the exam fees through PSI add approximately $100–$150 per part. Combined with a surety bond premium and background check, total costs typically land in the $400–$600 range.
    • North Carolina — The North Carolina Licensing Board for General Contractors charges a $75 application fee and $100 examination fee. The bond requirement and background check push total costs into the $450–$650 range depending on bond premium rates.
    • Virginia — Virginia's Board for Contractors charges $100 for the initial application and $100 for the exam. The state requires a minimum $50,000 surety bond for Class A contractors (projects over $120,000), making the bond premium a significant portion of the total cost. Total first-year costs typically run $500–$750.
    • Tennessee — Tennessee's Board for Licensing Contractors charges $200 for the application and requires a financial statement and surety bond scaled to the contractor's project-value classification. A contractor in the mid-range classification can expect total costs of $500–$700.
    • Utah — Utah's Division of Occupational and Professional Licensing charges moderate fees, and the exam is administered through PSI. Total costs including the surety bond and background check typically fall in the $450–$650 range.

    The most expensive states

    At the top of the cost spectrum, a handful of states impose substantially higher fees — driven by larger bond requirements, more expensive exams, higher application fees, or all three.

    High-cost states ($800–$1,500+ total)

    • California — California is consistently one of the most expensive states to get licensed in. The CSLB charges a $450 application fee (which covers the first exam sitting), a $200–$350 initial license fee depending on entity type, and $49 for fingerprinting. The $25,000 surety bond runs approximately $250–$750 in annual premium. Total first-year costs: $950–$1,600 depending on entity type and bond rate. See our full California general contractor guide for the complete breakdown.
    • Florida — Florida's Construction Industry Licensing Board charges $249 for the initial application, and the exam fees through Prometric add $100–$200 per part. The state requires both a $10,000 surety bond and proof of financial stability (a financial statement or additional bonding). Total first-year costs: $600–$1,100.
    • Arizona — Arizona's Registrar of Contractors charges a $200–$500 application fee depending on license classification, plus exam fees. The dual-license structure (residential vs. commercial) means contractors who want both must pay twice. Bond requirements scale with project value. Total costs can reach $800–$1,200 for a dual-licensed contractor.
    • Nevada — Nevada's State Contractors Board charges a $300 application fee, $150 exam fee, and requires a minimum $50,000 surety bond (premium: $500–$1,500 depending on credit). Add the financial statement requirement and background check, and total first-year costs reach $1,000–$2,000, making Nevada one of the most expensive jurisdictions in the country.
    • Hawaii — Hawaii's Contractors License Board charges $75 for the application and $50 for the exam, but the surety bond requirement of $25,000 combined with the state's higher insurance costs and mandatory workers' compensation push total out-of-pocket into the $800–$1,200 range.

    The hidden cost: renewal fees add up over time

    The initial licensing cost is a one-time expense, but renewal fees are permanent. Every state requires periodic license renewal — typically every one to three years — and the cumulative cost of renewals over a 20- or 30-year career substantially exceeds the initial licensing cost.

    Consider the following examples:

    • California: $450 renewal every two years (sole owner). Over 20 years: $4,500 in renewal fees alone.
    • Florida: Approximately $200–$300 per two-year renewal cycle plus continuing education costs. Over 20 years: $2,000–$3,000.
    • Virginia: $100–$200 per two-year renewal. Over 20 years: $1,000–$2,000.
    • North Carolina: $100–$200 per year. Over 20 years: $2,000–$4,000.

    And renewal fees are just the government portion. The surety bond must be maintained annually, general liability and workers' compensation premiums continue for the life of the business, and many states require continuing education credits that carry their own tuition costs.

    Why the cheapest state is not always the best deal

    It is tempting to look at this list and conclude that contractors in low-cost states have it easy. In reality, the cost of a license tells you almost nothing about the difficulty of obtaining it or the value it delivers.

    A state with low fees but a four-year experience requirement is not necessarily easier to enter than a state with high fees but a two-year requirement. A state with no statewide license may seem inexpensive on paper, but it means contractors in that state lack the portability benefits of a state-issued credential — making it harder to take advantage of reciprocity agreements or the NASCLA accredited exam.

    The states with the highest licensing costs also tend to have the strongest consumer-protection frameworks, the most active enforcement against unlicensed operators, and the highest barriers to entry — which means less competition for contractors who do hold a valid license. In California, where the CSLB aggressively prosecutes unlicensed activity and the market for licensed contractors is tightly regulated, holding a Class B license is a genuine competitive advantage that commands premium pricing.

    How to reduce your licensing costs

    While most licensing fees are fixed and non-negotiable, there are a few strategies that can reduce total out-of-pocket costs:

    • Improve your credit score before applying for a surety bond. Bond premiums are directly tied to personal credit. A contractor with a 750+ credit score may pay 1% of the bond amount annually, while a contractor with a 600 score may pay 5% or more for the same bond. On a $25,000 bond, that is the difference between a $250 and a $1,250 annual premium.
    • Use the NASCLA exam to avoid multiple state exam fees. If you plan to work in more than one participating state, a single $395 NASCLA exam fee can replace multiple state-specific exam fees of $100–$300 each. See our NASCLA exam guide for details.
    • Pass the exam on the first attempt. Retake fees add up quickly — $100 to $300 per sitting, plus the indirect cost of delayed licensing. Invest in proper study materials upfront.
    • Structure your business entity thoughtfully. Some states charge different fees for sole proprietors, partnerships, LLCs, and corporations. In California, for example, the initial license fee for a sole owner is $200, while a corporation pays $350. Choose the entity structure that makes sense for your business, but be aware of the licensing cost differential.

    Licensing is an investment, not an expense

    Contractor licensing costs in the United States are wildly inconsistent. A general contractor in one state can get licensed for under $200, while a contractor doing the same work in a neighboring state might spend $1,500 or more. The fees reflect each state's regulatory philosophy — some states view licensing as a minimal registration process, while others treat it as a comprehensive gatekeeping mechanism with correspondingly higher costs.

    Regardless of what your state charges, licensing is a business investment, not an expense. The contractors who treat it as an expense — cutting corners, skipping renewals, or operating without a license to save a few hundred dollars — are the ones who end up paying far more when a client dispute, an insurance claim, or a state enforcement action catches up with them.
