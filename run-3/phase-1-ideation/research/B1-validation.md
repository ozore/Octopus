## B1 — Article 14 (CRA Exploit Clock): deep validation

### 1. The regulation says what the card says — and the card under-reads it

I pulled the CRA text from EUR-Lex today (HTTP 200, 712,338 bytes). **Article 14** is verbatim as described, with more structure than the card models:

- **Art 14(1)/(2)** — actively exploited vulnerability: (a) **early warning ≤24h** of becoming aware, naming Member States where the product was made available; (b) **vulnerability notification ≤72h** (general nature of exploit and vulnerability, corrective/mitigating measures, sensitivity flag); (c) **final report no later than 14 days after a corrective or mitigating measure is available**.
- **Art 14(3)/(4)** — severe incident: 24h early warning (incl. whether unlawful/malicious acts are suspected), 72h incident notification, final report **within one month after the 72h notification** — a different deadline the card's "24h/72h/14-day decision tree" omits entirely.
- **Art 14(6)** — the coordinating CSIRT may demand an intermediate report. **Art 14(8)** — a parallel duty to inform impacted users. Neither appears in the card.
- **Art 14(7)** — routing is by *main establishment* ("where the decisions related to the cybersecurity of its products are predominantly taken"), with a four-step fallback for non-EU manufacturers (authorised rep → importer → distributor → most users). Per-customer configuration the product must get right or it drafts to the wrong CSIRT.

**Manufacturer** (Art 3(13)): whoever develops/manufactures products with digital elements or has them made, and markets them under its own name or trademark, "whether for payment, monetisation or **free of charge**." **Product with digital elements** (Art 3(10)) includes components placed on the market separately; Recital 87 confirms vulnerability-handling duties "apply to products with digital elements in their entirety, including to all integrated components."

**Dates** (Art 71(2)): applies from 11 December 2027, "**However, Article 14 shall apply from 11 September 2026** and Chapter IV (Articles 35 to 51) shall apply from 11 June 2026." **Art 69(3)** is the card's strongest verified claim: Article 14 applies to **all** in-scope products "placed on the market before 11 December 2027" — the entire back catalogue. Commission guidance para 210 adds that reporting duties **survive end-of-support**, while vulnerability-handling duties do not.

**Penalties** (Art 64(2)): Annex I plus **Articles 13 and 14** carry fines "up to EUR 15 000 000 or, if the offender is an undertaking, up to 2,5 % of the its total worldwide annual turnover, whichever is higher." Card correct.

### 2. Three findings that break the pitch

**(a) The EUR 15M anchor does not apply to most of the stated ICP.** Art 64(10)(a): administrative fines "shall not apply to … manufacturers that qualify as microenterprises or small enterprises with regard to any failure to meet the deadline referred to in Article 14(2), point (a), or Article 14(4), point (a)" — i.e. the 24-hour early warning specifically. Art 3(63) binds this to Recommendation 2003/361/EC (small = <50 staff). The card targets "10–200 person" vendors and prices "anchored against a EUR 15M downside." For the 10–49 band, the downside on the exact obligation the product automates is **zero fine**. The anchor survives only for the 72h/final stages and Art 13/Annex I.

**(b) There is no monitoring obligation — the product manufactures the liability it sells against.** Commission FAQ 5.1 lists ways a manufacturer may become aware, then states: "**It should be noted that this does not imply that the manufacturer is required to carry out such activities or monitor such channels to comply with the reporting obligations.**" Article 14 is awareness-triggered, not surveillance-mandated. An hourly KEV/OSV cron against your shipped SBOMs is therefore a machine for *creating* awareness — and with it 24-hour clocks that would not otherwise have started. That is a genuinely hostile sales conversation with a CTO, and it is the opposite of the card's framing.

**(c) The clock cannot be started by code — engine-never-arbiter is violated at the core.** Commission Guidance para 213 (final guidance, C(2026) 5252, published 27 July 2026): the manufacturer "is to be regarded as having become aware when, **after such an initial assessment, it has a reasonable degree of certainty** that … a vulnerability contained in its product with digital elements is being actively exploited." Para 214: "the point in time … will depend on the circumstances." And FAQ 5.4 is explicit that an SBOM∩KEV hit is *not* sufficient: "If the manufacturer … is aware that an integrated component contains a vulnerability, **but that vulnerability cannot be exploited in its product with digital elements, that vulnerability is not actively exploited, and therefore it is not subject to mandatory reporting.**" (Conversely, where it *is* exploitable, 5.4 confirms both the integrator and the component manufacturer must notify — the card's component thesis is right.)

So the honest product cannot say "your 24-hour clock started at 04:00 UTC." It can only say "candidate — assess promptly." The card's A3 already gestures at this for *fuzzy* matches, but the constraint applies to **every** match, including exact PURL hits. The headline promise ("your clock is already running") asserts a legal determination the engine is not permitted to make.

### 3. The ENISA platform: real, dated, and closed

ENISA's own SRP page and FAQ (both HTTP 200 today) confirm the platform is **not yet live**, targeted at 11 September 2026, access via **EU Login** with CSIRT validation of registered representatives. FAQ Q16 publishes the actual field schema — 26 fields, X/O/C/I coded across 24h/72h/Final (notification type, manufacturer, product, product type per Annex III/IV, Member States, CVE ID, EUVD ID, general nature of vulnerability/exploit, sensitivity, severity, malicious actor, etc.). Good news: the drafting target is small and public. Fatal news: FAQ Q15 — "Organisations might automate reporting workflows … **however no Application Programming Interfaces will be provided at this stage.**" The product can never file, never confirm receipt, never hold the evidence chain end-to-end. It stops at a copy-paste package.

### 4. Who is worried, and what is already sold

Hacker News: 112 CRA stories all-time; every high-engagement one (215, 183, 151, 137, 126 pts) is 2022–23 open-source-burden outrage. With the obligation **23 days away**, the 2026 stories score 6 and 5 points with zero comments. Live comments (175) are political, not operational ("EU just killed all hardware startups", 2026-07-23).

The competitive picture is the killer. **CVD Portal** (cvdportal.com, live, HTTP 200, posted to HN 2026-07-13) ships this exact product at nearly this exact price: **Free** tier, **€99/mo** "Article 14 notification workflow (24h / 72h / 14d)" + "SRP-ready submission package" + SBOM registry (SPDX/CycloneDX) + NVD/EUVD feeds, **€299/mo** Compliance, **€1,499/mo** Enterprise with "Automated SBOM ↔ CVE supply chain alerts." Its free no-signup classification tool *is* the card's proposed wedge, already running. It independently states "ENISA exposes no manufacturer submission API." Alongside it: craevidence.com (live, custom pricing, consulting-led), cra-facts.com, OpenCRA (free templates), and sbomify at **$159/mo billed annually** with a "CRA Compliance Wizard" (card's $159 figure verified, annual-billing caveat noted).

### 5. Data layer

CISA KEV verified live today: HTTP 200, 1,592,987 bytes, `catalogVersion 2026.08.19`, `dateReleased 2026-08-19T17:00:32Z`, 1,671 entries, Last-Modified matching. OSV.dev v1 returns 200/JSON. ENISA's EUVD API endpoint returned HTML, not JSON — needs work despite EUVD ID being a schema field.

### 6. Verdict

The regulation is exactly as described and the deadline is real. But the three legs of the thesis each fail on primary sources: the fine does not bite the target segment on the automated obligation, no duty to monitor exists, and the clock-start is a judgement call the engine may not make. Add a price-matched competitor already shipping the identical wedge and a closed filing channel, and the remaining product is a candidate-surfacing tool with a copy-paste output, sold into a segment facing no fine for the thing it automates.

# B1 — Article 14 (CRA Exploit Clock): Deep Validation

**Verdict: the data plumbing works and is cheap; the *product thesis* does not survive contact with the regulation's own text.** Two findings are disqualifying-grade, and both were established from primary sources today.

## 1. The corpus is real, free, and hourly-cron-trivial

Every feed in the card is live and keyless. ## 2. The matching problem is much worse than "purl vs CPE fuzziness"

**KEV carries no purl, no CPE, and no version ranges at all.** `vendorProject` and `product` are free text ("Multiple Products", "IOS and IOS XE Software"). So "component X version Y is in KEV" is *never* computed from KEV directly; it requires a CVE→package bridge through OSV/GHSA.

I measured that bridge exhaustively rather than sampling. Downloading 11 OSV ecosystem exports (~373 MB) and intersecting every KEV CVE against advisory IDs, aliases and `related`:

| Path | KEV CVEs resolved |
|---|---|
| Language ecosystems (npm, Maven, PyPI, Go, crates.io, NuGet, RubyGems, Packagist, Hex) | 128 |
| Debian:12 / Debian:13 | 175 |
| Ubuntu:24.04:LTS | 64 |
| Alpine:v3.20 | 10 |
| **Union** | **278 / 1671 = 16.6%** |

**83.4% of CISA KEV maps to no package identifier anywhere** — it is Microsoft Windows, Cisco IOS, Adobe, Ivanti, Fortinet, VMware, SolarWinds: proprietary products nameable only as prose. Against an SBOM, those entries are permanently unmatchable.

The fuzziness is also *structural, not just semantic*. I hit a live instance and must report it: `Debian/all.zip` yields **0** KEV matches while `Debian:12/all.zip` yields **175** — the unversioned bucket is not the union. `Ubuntu` records are keyed `UBUNTU-CVE-2002-2439` with `aliases: null`, so alias-joining silently returns zero unless you parse the CVE out of the ID. I got this wrong on my first pass and caught it only by spot-checking Log4Shell. A production matcher will hit this class of trap repeatedly, and it fails *silently green* — the worst possible failure mode for a compliance clock. Note also that nearly every KEV CVE has *some* GHSA ID (1,662 of 1,678 EUVD-exploited entries carry one), but only `github_reviewed` advisories carry purls; the unreviewed NVD-mirrored ones do not. "Has a GHSA" ≠ "is matchable".

**Base rate.** KEV added 15 entries across 3–19 Aug 2026 (~0.88/day). Apply 16.6% and the whole world sees roughly **one purl-resolvable KEV addition every six days** — ~53/year across all open-source packages in existence. A single customer's dependency tree will trigger this **zero to two times a year**. A product whose headline event fires ~never is a churn machine, regardless of the deadline.

## 3. Fatal: the trigger the product computes is not the trigger the law defines

Article 3(42) (EUR-Lex, verbatim): *"'actively exploited vulnerability' means a vulnerability for which there is reliable evidence that a malicious actor has exploited it in a system without permission of the system owner"*. Recital 68 narrows it decisively: *"Actively exploited vulnerabilities concern instances where **a manufacturer establishes that a security breach affecting its users** or any other natural or legal persons **has resulted from a malicious actor making use of a flaw in one of the products** with digital elements made available on the market by the manufacturer."*

Article 14(1) attaches the duty to a vulnerability *"contained in the product… that it becomes aware of"*. A KEV listing establishes that a CVE is exploited **somewhere in the world, in someone's deployment**. It does not establish that a breach affecting *this manufacturer's* users resulted from *this manufacturer's* product. These are different predicates, and the gap is the manufacturer's own factual determination.

So the promise — *"your clock is already running"* — has the engine asserting a legal conclusion. The card's A3 defence handles only the *fuzzy* match ("Possible match, not clock-starting"); on an **exact** purl match the design auto-starts a 24-hour statutory clock and drafts a filing. That is precisely the engine-never-arbiter line: the product would be manufacturing a reporting obligation, with EUR 15,000,000 or 2.5% of worldwide turnover (Art. 64(2)) on the other side of being wrong in either direction. Over-trigger and the customer files needlessly; under-trigger and the product implied cover it never had.

## 4. Timing and competition are worse than the card assumes

Art. 71(2) is confirmed verbatim: *"This Regulation shall apply from 11 December 2027. However, Article 14 shall apply from 11 September 2026"* — 23 days out. But **ENISA's Single Reporting Platform is not live**, and ENISA states plainly that **"no Application Programming Interfaces will be provided at this stage"** — submission is an EU Login web form. ENISA *does* publish the field table (FAQ Q16), and it deflates the drafting feature: at the 24-hour stage only *notification type, level, manufacturer name, product, title* and the malicious-act flag are obligatory; **CVE/EUVD ID is merely "Optional"**. The generated artifact is a six-field form. The judgment-heavy fields (severity, impact, malicious-actor detail, security-update detail) are all 72h/final and are exactly what an engine must not invent.

Two more live findings. **EUVD** — ENISA's own database, which the card omits — is live and keyless (`/api/search?exploited=true` → `{items,total}`, **total 1678**), carries `exploitedSince`, `epss` and aliases, and is the EU-native citation the SRP form asks for. But its exploited list is **99.6% a mirror of CISA KEV (1671 of 1678 shared CVEs)** — there is no independent EU exploit signal to arbitrage. And **sbomify now ships a "CRA Compliance Wizard" in its $159/mo Business tier** (Community $0, Enterprise custom): the incumbent the card dismissed as "an artifact hub, not a clock" has already moved onto this ground.

## Recommendation

Kill the clock; keep the archive. The defensible, deterministic, A3-clean product is the **per-release SBOM archive plus an exploit-adjacency watch** that says "a component in v2.3.1 that you shipped to the EU appears in CISA KEV as of <timestamp>; here is the evidence trail; *you* determine whether Art. 14 applies." That is a monitoring and evidence product, not an arbiter. It survives the recital-68 problem, survives the 16.6% match ceiling (because low event volume is fine for an archive but fatal for an alerting product), and it is the half of the thesis nobody can retroactively rebuild for the customer. The 24-hour clock should be a countdown the *customer* starts.

## 1. The regulation holds — verified against primary text, not secondary

Fetched the consolidated Regulation (EU) 2024/2847 HTML from EUR-Lex (712,856 B, HTTP 200). ## 3. What the free OSS substitute actually does — and does not

This is the strongest finding for the candidate. OWASP Dependency-Track (Apache-2.0, latest **v4.14.3, released 2026-07-20** per its own changelog) ingests NVD, GHSA, OSV and has native **EPSS** support (v4.12.0 added EPSS policy conditions; v4.14.0 added EPSS for GHSA advisories). It does **not** natively ingest **CISA KEV**. Issue #2267 ("Add support for the CISA KEV catalog") has been open since **2022-12-10**; discussion #5461 shows a user requesting KEV on **2025-10-30 explicitly to "comply with the CRA reporting requirement"**, a maintainer replying **2025-11-12** "There is [a plan], see #2267", and another +1 on **2026-06-05**. Nothing shipped.

So the free stack today gives you *probabilistic* exploit signal (EPSS) but not the *binary, dated, authoritative* signal Article 14 turns on. A determined engineer can wire KEV in with a cron and a PowerShell script (the DT community docs point at exactly that) — but that is a build, and it produces no dated evidence chain and no ENISA-shaped artifact. **The gap is real, narrow, and closing.**

## 4. Where a defensible $/mo sits — Ramanujam/Poyar

**Value metric.** The card proposes *products × shipped versions under watch*. Nobody in the segment meters versions: sbomify meters products×components, FOSSA meters projects, Snyk/Socket/Aikido meter developers. Versions-under-watch is an expansion axis that grows with shipping cadence and is therefore anti-correlated with value — a five-person team on trunk-based deploys burns 25 versions in a fortnight while carrying one product's worth of legal exposure. **Recommend: meter on *products with digital elements under watch* — the CRA's own legal unit (Art. 14 obligations attach per product) — with versions as generous fair use, not the meter.** This is the rare case where the regulation hands you the value metric.

**Price ceiling.** Willingness to pay is bounded above not by the €15M fine (nobody prices off a tail risk they don't believe applies to them) but by the next-cheapest way to look compliant, which is $159/mo. **The card's $99 Solo is priced below the reference and destroys the anchor** — it invites the "isn't this just sbomify minus the SBOM hub?" comparison and it prices a deadline product like a hobby tool. **$299 Team is correctly placed** (top of the observed self-serve band, below Aikido Basic at $300 and Acuna at ~CHF 449). **$799 Fleet is above every published self-serve price in the segment**; it is defensible only if it carries the filing artifact plus the customer-facing distribution portal, and it should be sold as the tier where you stop retyping.

**Packaging.** Recommended ladder: **$149 Watch** (detection + evidence log, one product — deliberately *at* the reference price, not under it), **$399 Report** (the 24/72/14-day artifact generator, multi-product, webhook/PagerDuty, immutable attestation log), **$899 Fleet**. Put the packaging boundary exactly where the card says — "does it produce the filing artifact" — because that is the only step where a buyer can see what they get for 2.7×.

## 5. Engine-never-arbiter strain — three places it bends

1. **"Starts the CRA 24-hour clock" is the product asserting a legal fact.** Art. 14(2)(a) starts the clock when the *manufacturer* becomes aware of an actively exploited vulnerability *in its product*. A KEV entry is CISA's determination about a CVE, not a determination about the customer's product. The engine may present *"KEV added CVE-X at 17:00:32Z; version 3.2.1 contains component Z"*; the customer must press **Acknowledge awareness** to start a timer they own. Anything else makes a model's match the trigger for a €15M-exposed obligation.
2. **LLM-written "narrative fields"** are precisely Art. 14(2)(b)'s "general nature of the exploit … and any corrective or mitigating measures taken" — i.e. the substance of a regulatory filing. Competitor CMA Testing launched exactly this on **2026-06-25** ("AI compliance assistant automatically generates ENISA-compliant … early warning, detailed report, final report"). Do not match it. Assemble the skeleton deterministically, inject verified facts, leave the judgement fields blank and required.
3. **The free scope checker** determines applicability, a legal conclusion. Deterministic decision tree with regulation citations only; no generation. Note also that **cra-facts.com already gives away a free Scope & Class Checker, Obligations Checker and SBOM tool**, so this wedge is not novel.

## 6. Timing risk that pricing cannot fix

**ENISA's Single Reporting Platform is not live.** ENISA's own page (fetched today) publishes only a Factsheet v1.0 (July 2026) and three Assigned-Representative guides updated 3–14 August 2026, and states the platform becomes mandatory "11 September 2026 onwards". **No API or machine-to-machine submission is documented anywhere on ENISA's page.** The product therefore cannot close the loop; it produces a document a human retypes into a portal that does not yet exist. That is exactly where the card locates its value step, and it is currently a paper step.

## 04 Kill-thesis

## B1 — Article 14 (CRA Exploit Clock): kill-thesis validation

**Verdict: the deadline is real; the product is not.** Five findings, each from primary text fetched today, would individually wound this candidate. Together they invert its core promise. The strongest attack is not that the product fails — it is that the product *increases the legal exposure of the customer it sells to*.

### 1. There is no duty to monitor — and buying this creates the liability (fatal)

The Commission's own CRA implementation FAQ (§5.1, doc 122331, updated 2 July 2026) states the CRA "does not specify how a manufacturer is to become aware," lists channels by which awareness may arise, then adds the decisive sentence: *"It should be noted that this does not imply that the manufacturer is required to carry out such activities or monitor such channels to comply with the reporting obligations."*

Article 14(1) obliges notification only of a vulnerability the manufacturer "becomes aware of." The card's premise — an hourly KEV/OSV/GHSA watch so you catch it — sells compliance with a duty that does not exist. Worse, it manufactures the trigger: subscribing converts a non-obligation into a running 24-hour clock plus a timestamped, discoverable record proving the manufacturer knew. Every Commission example of "becoming aware" is inbound (customer, researcher, government agency, own telemetry); none is "an SCA feed matched a CVE to my SBOM." The rational cost-minimising posture for an in-scope SME is *not to buy this*. That is adverse selection no messaging fixes, because the honest pitch is "we start your clock earlier and prove you knew."

### 2. A KEV∩SBOM match cannot satisfy the statutory test (fatal to the "clock")

Article 3(42): an actively exploited vulnerability is one "for which there is reliable evidence that a malicious actor has exploited it in a system without permission of the system owner." Article 14(1) narrows this to a vulnerability "**contained in the product** with digital elements."

The brief asked whether a tool can know the "exploited in MY product" test. Primary answer: it cannot. FAQ §5.4 confirms component-level exploitation *can* flow through — "where the product … contains an actively exploited vulnerability originating from an integrated component, the manufacturer … is required to notify" — but immediately supplies the dispositive carve-out: *"If the manufacturer … is aware that an integrated component contains a vulnerability, but that vulnerability **cannot be exploited in its product**, that vulnerability is not actively exploited, and therefore it is not subject to mandatory reporting."* FAQ §5.2 is stronger still, framing the trigger as the manufacturer establishing "that a security breach … has resulted from a malicious actor making use of a flaw in one of the products … made available on the market by the manufacturer."

So the governing test is **reachability/exploitability in the specific product**, plus evidence of a breach through that product. KEV establishes neither. A KEV∩SBOM hit is a *candidate*, and resolving it is an engineering judgment the product cannot make. The card's A3 half-anticipates this ("Possible match, not clock-starting"), but the honest degraded mode collapses the deliverable to a ranked triage list — which is a vulnerability scanner, the exact thing the card says the job is *not* ("not 'give me a vulnerability scanner'").

### 3. The deliverable is six form fields, and there is no API to file them into

ENISA's SRP FAQ Q16 publishes the field table. At the 24-hour stage only six fields are obligatory: notification type, notification level, manufacturer name, product, title, and (incidents only) suspected-malicious flag. **CVE ID is merely optional at 24h.** There is nothing to "draft" — a human fills this in under two minutes. The fields that need real writing are all at 72h: general nature of the vulnerability, general nature of the exploit, "corrective or mitigating measures taken," and measures users can take. Those are manufacturer-only facts an external feed-watcher cannot know; the LLM would be inventing them.

ENISA FAQ Q15 closes the loop: *"no Application Programming Interfaces will be provided at this stage."* Submission is a human logging into EU Login and re-keying. The product cannot file, cannot pre-populate, cannot even round-trip a draft. It is a clipboard.

### 4. The €15M anchor does not apply to the target segment

Article 64(2) sets the EUR 15,000,000 / 2.5% fines for Article 14. But Article 64(10)(a) derogates: those fines "shall not apply to … manufacturers that qualify as microenterprises or small enterprises with regard to any failure to meet the deadline referred to in Article 14(2), point (a)" — i.e. the 24-hour early warning. Recital 133 states the same intent plainly. Under Recommendation 2003/361/EC a small enterprise is <50 staff and ≤EUR 10m. The card targets 10–200 person vendors; for everyone under 50 the fear-anchor for *this specific deadline* is legally zero. (Note a genuine drafting quirk: 64(10) says "by way of derogation from paragraphs 3 to 9," while the Article 14 fines sit in paragraph 2 — the recital resolves the intent, but the pricing story rests on a cross-reference ambiguity.)

### 5. The alert fires approximately never

Live KEV today: catalogVersion 2026.08.19, 1,671 entries, of which 271 were added in the last twelve months — roughly five per week *across every vendor on earth*, dominated by Microsoft (385), Cisco (96), Apple (94), Adobe (80). On my own vendor-string classification of the live file, only 123 of 1,671 entries (7.4%) come from OSS-library-style projects, and just 15 in the past year. For one customer's dependency tree the expected KEV∩SBOM hit rate is a small fraction of one event per year. A $99–799/mo subscription whose headline event never occurs is insurance — and the card's own sibling entry concedes insurance churns without a day-to-day artifact. The per-release SBOM archive is passive; it is not that artifact.

### 6. Market and scope

TAM is narrower than written. FAQ §1.2: "standalone Software-as-a-Service (SaaS) or other cloud solutions designed and developed outside the responsibility of a manufacturer of a product with digital elements are not themselves products with digital elements." Pure SaaS is out of scope, removing much of "10–200 person software vendors" and leaving embedded/IoT/firmware/on-prem/SDK vendors — a smaller, more hardware-shaped, more sales-led segment. Meanwhile sbomify already ships a "CRA Compliance Wizard" at $159/mo Business, and Black Duck already markets CRA Article 14 reporting (contact-sales). The wedge is a feature inside tools the buyer already has.

### Engine-never-arbiter

Two strains. (a) The 72h narrative fields ("general nature of the exploit," "corrective or mitigating measures taken") are facts the model cannot know; generating them is fabrication in a regulatory filing. (b) Article 14(7) requires selecting the CSIRT coordinator by "main establishment," defined as where cybersecurity decisions "are predominantly taken" — a legal characterisation, with a four-step fallback ladder. Any product that picks the recipient authority is making a legal determination. Both must be customer-asserted inputs, not engine outputs.

### Timing

The deadline is confirmed (Article 69: reporting applies 11 September 2026). But ENISA's own FAQ says the SRP public URL "will be communicated and published in due course on this page before the platform goes live" — as of today, 23 days out, no URL is published and a testing period is only "expected." Building to an unpublished schema, with no API and a possibly-unbuilt platform, is not a panic-window advantage; it is schedule risk on someone else's project.

**Recommendation: kill, or reduce to the SBOM archive.** The one asset that survives — an immutable per-release SBOM store no competitor can backfill — is real but is a $19/mo commodity, not the promise on the card.

### Fatal risks

- Negative-value core loop: the CRA imposes no duty to monitor (Commission FAQ §5.1), so the product's central function does not create compliance — it creates awareness, starting a 24h clock and generating a timestamped, discoverable record that the customer knew. A cost-minimising in-scope SME is better off not subscribing. This is not a messaging problem; it is the product's mechanism.
- The statutory trigger is unreachable from the data: FAQ §5.4 exempts component vulnerabilities that 'cannot be exploited in its product', and §5.2 frames the trigger as a breach through the manufacturer's own product. KEV∩SBOM proves neither. The honest output is a candidate list requiring human reachability analysis — i.e. a vulnerability scanner, the exact positioning the card disclaims.
- The deliverable is trivial and unfileable: only six obligatory fields at 24h (CVE ID optional), and ENISA states 'no Application Programming Interfaces will be provided at this stage'. There is no draft worth $99–799/mo and no path to submit one; the customer re-keys into EU Login by hand.
- The pricing anchor is void for the target segment: Art. 64(10)(a) exempts micro and small enterprises (<50 staff, ≤EUR 10m) from any administrative fine for missing the 24-hour deadline. The EUR 15M downside cannot be sold to the majority of a '10–200 person vendor' ICP.
- Event frequency near zero: 1,671 KEV entries total, 271 added in the trailing year, overwhelmingly commercial network/OS vendors. A single customer's expected trigger rate is a fraction of one per year — an insurance product with no day-to-day artifact to carry retention, which the card's own sibling entry identifies as a churn death sentence.
- TAM narrower than stated: standalone SaaS is out of CRA scope (FAQ §1.2), removing much of the 'software vendors' ICP and leaving embedded/IoT/firmware/on-prem vendors — a smaller, more sales-led, more hardware-shaped segment than a self-serve GitHub-App motion addresses.
- Engine-never-arbiter strain: the 72h fields ('general nature of the exploit', 'corrective or mitigating measures taken') are manufacturer-only facts a feed-watcher cannot know — generating them is fabrication inside a regulatory filing; and Art. 14(7) CSIRT selection turns on 'main establishment', a legal characterisation the product must never compute.
- Schedule dependency on an unfinished government system: 23 days from the obligation date, ENISA has not published the SRP URL, the testing period is only 'expected', no API is planned, and Art. 14(10) makes a format implementing act discretionary ('may'). Building the 'ENISA-format draft generator' means building to a spec that does not yet exist.
